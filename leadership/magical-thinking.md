# Magical Thinking in Engineering Leadership

Bjorn Roche (July 2026) names a pattern: the belief that a leader can dramatically raise a
team's productivity through **"one weird trick"** — an inspiring speech, growth-mindset
rhetoric, or adopting AI — without addressing the operational problems underneath.

The value of the piece is the **naming**, not the individual cautions, most of which are held
elsewhere in these notes. It is a short, experience-based essay; the only cited evidence is a
footnoted reference to psychological-safety research.

## What makes it one category

The contribution is noticing that three things usually treated separately are the same move:

| The trick | The substitution |
|---|---|
| The inspiring speech | Rhetoric in place of direction |
| "This team can achieve anything" | Unbounded faith in place of resource allocation |
| Adopting AI tooling | Procurement in place of process change |

Each promises a step change in output without anyone having to do the difficult, unglamorous
work — understanding constraints, allocating finite resources deliberately, and removing
bottlenecks. Having one label for the category is more useful than three separate warnings,
because the tell is the same in each case: *the productivity gain is asserted, and no
operational change is proposed alongside it.*

Roche's replacements are deliberately deflationary:

- Not "ambitious goals inspire teams" but clear guidance on what the resource priorities are
- Not "the team can achieve anything" but "the team achieves when its bottlenecks are removed"
- Not hiring "10xers" but improving team coordination and communication

## Why the pattern survives failing

The sharpest observation, and the one not otherwise recorded here:

**Magical thinking absolves the leader of responsibility.** If productivity was supposed to
come from belief or from a tool, then when it doesn't arrive the available explanation is
*insufficient effort* — the team didn't want it enough, didn't adopt the tool properly, wasn't
bought in. The diagnosis lands on the team rather than on the system, so the failure never
falsifies the approach and the same move can be repeated.

That is what makes it a trap rather than merely a mistake. An ordinary bad plan produces
evidence against itself; this one produces evidence against the people executing it.

## The caveat, which matters

Roche is not arguing against inspiration. Inspiring speeches are described as genuinely
powerful **when they reinforce practices that already exist** — the failure mode is
substitution, not enthusiasm. A leader who has removed the bottlenecks and then rallies the
team is doing something real; a leader who rallies instead is not.
