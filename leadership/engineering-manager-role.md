# The Engineering Manager Role

James Samuel frames the engineering manager as existing to **amplify the collective
impact** of a team by removing the obstacles to its effectiveness. Management layers
are not overhead to be flattened away: as an organisation grows, someone has to
coordinate communication, prioritisation, and alignment, and that coordination does
not happen for free. He organises the role into four pillars.

## The four pillars

| Pillar | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **People Leadership** | Hiring, onboarding, career development, and culture. Reading individual motivation (some engineers want autonomy, others want structure); coaching, conflict resolution, and delivering feedback. |
| **Technical Leadership** | Guiding architectural decisions and technical health *without necessarily being the strongest coder* — asking the right questions, balancing elegance against business value, and managing technical debt. |
| **Product Leadership** | Partnering with product management so the team solves the *right* problem; bridging customer value and technical feasibility; helping evaluate scope and validate assumptions before a major investment. |
| **Delivery Leadership** | The "operating system for software development" — the process, planning, and communication that turn ideas into shipped software; removing friction, managing uncertainty, and keeping information flowing between engineers, product, and stakeholders. |

## How the pillars balance

- **The balance shifts with context.** A platform migration leans on technical
  leadership; a period of rapid growth leans on people leadership. No fixed weighting
  is correct across situations.
- **The framework matters less than the outcome.** Samuel's caution is that the
  taxonomy is a lens, not a scorecard — what matters is whether the manager's
  processes actually help the team deliver quality software.
- **The core job is obstacle removal.** Underneath all four pillars, the manager's
  recurring task is to identify what is preventing the team from succeeding and clear it.

## The day-to-day activities

A companion piece by the same author covers the other half of the role: if the four
pillars are what a manager is *responsible for*, these six are what the job actually
consists of, hour to hour. Both are drawn from experience rather than research, and
no time allocation across them is offered.

| Activity | |
|---|---|
| **Information gathering** | Collecting context on people, process, and product, across the team and the wider organisation |
| **Information sharing** | Synthesising it and getting it to whoever needs it to decide |
| **Decision making** | Choosing on priorities, people, and technical direction under incomplete information — see [Facilitating Technical Decisions](decision-facilitation.md) |
| **Influencing decisions** | Shaping outcomes on teams you don't control — see [Managing Sideways](managing-sideways.md) and [Principal Engineer Influence](principal-engineer-influence.md) |
| **Leading and setting direction** | Defining the path to a shared goal and modelling the behaviour expected — see [Start with the Why](start-with-why.md) |
| **Planning** | Connecting the current state to the desired one in concrete steps — see [A Plan Is Not a Strategy](plan-is-not-a-strategy.md) |

Two things are worth pulling out of that list.

**The work is invisible.** Unlike an individual contributor's, a manager's output
leaves no artefact — there is no commit, no diff, no shipped feature attributable to
an hour spent gathering context or heading off a misalignment. The work is real and
consequential, but it cannot be pointed at, which is the source of much of the
difficulty in evaluating and defending the role.

**The first two are one pipeline.** Gathering and sharing are listed separately, but
in practice they are a single function: the manager as the **router** that moves
context between people who need it and cannot easily get it themselves. Framed that
way, information movement is not one-sixth of the job but a large fraction of it —
and it is precisely the part that leaves no trace.
