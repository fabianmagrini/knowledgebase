# Protecting Mavericks

**Mavericks** are independent thinkers and challengers of the status quo — often the source
of breakthroughs and a guard against organisational stagnation. Their unconventional
approach also creates friction. Protecting them is the management discipline of letting
their talent flourish without letting their disruption damage the team.

This is the *people* counterpart to [managed disruption](managed-disruption.md): managed
disruption channels change safely through process and architecture; protecting mavericks
channels it safely through individuals.

## Who they are

Non-conformist, purpose-driven, willing to take calculated risks and question established
norms. Frequently high performers. Their resistance to authority and conventional structure
can make them appear disruptive.

## The double-edged sword

- **Value:** they break groupthink, question assumptions, and surface game-changing ideas.
- **Cost:** disregard for process creates friction; blunt communication reads as abrasive;
  independence clashes with team-oriented environments.

## Strategies

- **Psychological safety** — make dissent safe; frame failure as learning.
- **Autonomy and ownership** — give freedom to explore; be flexible with rules that stifle
  creativity.
- **Clear goals and boundaries** — autonomy is not a free-for-all; align effort to strategy
  and contain negative spillover onto the team.
- **Support and recognition** — champion their contributions publicly so peers understand
  their role.
- **Coaching, not conformity** — develop communication and collaboration skills rather than
  forcing them into a mould.
- **Manage team dynamics** — handle the frustrations of colleagues who find their style
  challenging.
- **Trust-based relationships** — mavericks respond to managers they respect; openness and
  honesty are foundational.

## Staffing around them, and clearing the path

Phil McKinney's [innovation-team playbook](https://www.philmckinney.com/how-to-build-innovation-teams-my-playbook-from-hp-and-cablelabs/)
(September 2026), drawn from running HP's Innovation Program Office and CableLabs, adds two
things this note does not cover. His term for the maverick is the **radical**, and the same
protection argument applies — but he treats protecting them as insufficient on its own.

**The role taxonomy.** Radicals are one of four roles he recruits deliberately rather than
hoping talent assembles itself:

| Role | Function |
|---|---|
| **Visionary** | Holds the long-term picture of what could exist |
| **Leader** | Runs the team and the work — **deliberately a different person** |
| **Evangelist** | Sells the work internally, builds sponsorship, keeps the org bought in |
| **Radicals** | The unconventional thinkers this note is about |

The visionary/leader split is the useful part. Conflating them is the common default and it
fails in both directions: a visionary running delivery starves the vision, and a leader
expected to also be the visionary tends to narrow the ambition to what they can personally
manage. The **evangelist** has no equivalent anywhere in these notes, and it names work that
otherwise falls to whoever is least suited to it — the radical, who is usually the worst
internal salesperson for their own idea.

**Padlocks.** His word for the organisational barriers that block innovation, from an HP
anecdote about Bill Hewlett cutting the padlock off a tool room so engineers could get at the
parts. [The Engineering Manager Role](engineering-manager-role.md) already holds obstacle
removal as the manager's core job; "padlock" is a sharper noun for the specific case — a rule
or control that made sense for its original purpose and now mainly prevents work.

**"Adapt, don't adopt."** His named principle for why playbooks travel badly, with a worked
counterexample: Kroger copied HP's innovation playbook and it failed until it was localised for
a retail context. Worth naming because these notes *practise* it constantly — nearly every one
separates one organisation's implementation from the transferable part — without ever having a
label or evidence for it. His prescription is concrete: write the stage-gate questions in your
own organisation's vocabulary rather than importing someone else's.

**On his evidence.** He reports HP's Innovation Program Office making Fast Company's most-
innovative list three years running, and CableLabs moving from ~70 patents (1988–2012) to
1,000+ (2012–2026). Both are self-reported by the person who ran the programmes, and **patent
count is a contested proxy for innovation** — it measures filing activity, which responds to
policy and incentives independently of whether anything useful was invented. Read as his claim,
not as measurement. The piece also promotes a scorecard download, his book and a tools store.

## Bottom line

Protecting mavericks is not shielding them from all rules and consequences. It is building
an environment where unconventional talent flourishes for the benefit of the whole
organisation.
