# Coach and Judge

Jim Grey (August 2026) separates two things managers do with a struggling report and argues
they are different activities, not points on one continuum. **Coaching** develops someone whose
trajectory is upward. **Judging** states a standard, a deadline, and a consequence. The failure
mode he names is not managers who judge too readily — it is managers who stay in coaching mode
long after coaching has stopped changing anything.

His own framing of the limit, which the note should not overstate: *"None of this makes coaching
wrong. It just has a range."*

**Provenance.** A short practitioner essay from a software engineering leadership consultant. No
research, no data, no sponsorship. Its value is the five signals below; the surrounding argument
is asserted from experience.

## The five signals

The contribution worth keeping. Each is **observable behaviour rather than an assessment of
attitude or potential**, which is what makes them usable — they can be checked against the last
few months rather than argued about.

| Signal (Grey's words) | What it detects |
|---|---|
| "The explanation repeats." | The same reason is offered for a recurring gap — the account has stopped evolving |
| "You've named the same gap at least twice." | The problem is not that the person doesn't know |
| "Real support was offered and the trajectory didn't change." | Rules out the *manager didn't help* explanation |
| "The team is compensating." | The cost has moved off the individual and onto colleagues, where it is invisible in any review of that individual |
| "Your own language has started hedging." | The manager's own speech as instrument |

The last is the sharpest. Hedging — *he's really trying*, *she's been unlucky with the projects*
— is the manager narrating around a judgement they have already privately made. Grey's move is
to treat that vocabulary as data rather than as kindness.

The fourth deserves separate weight because it changes who bears the cost. Once colleagues route
around someone, the organisation is paying for the deferral in a currency that never appears in
the deferred conversation.

## What judge mode owes the person

Grey prescribes **clarity, not procedure**. The person should leave knowing:

- what must change
- what good looks like
- how long they have to demonstrate it
- what happens if they don't

He deliberately specifies no PIP, no documentation format, no timeline. The argument is that the
formal apparatus is downstream of, and often substituted for, a conversation in which those four
things were actually said out loud.

The case for this being kinder rather than harsher is that ambiguity is not neutral. Someone
held in indefinite coaching is denied both the information needed to improve and the information
needed to leave, and the team learns that the stated standard is decorative.

## Two claims to hold at arm's length

**The labour-market argument.** Grey suggests scarcity once made an underperformer feel less
risky than an empty seat — "replacing someone could take months" — and that "the market has
changed, and so has that tradeoff." This is asserted rather than evidenced, rests on a
particular reading of the 2026 market, and is the part of the piece most easily repurposed as
cover for cutting people. The five signals stand without it; the argument that clarity beats
ambiguity does not depend on replacement being cheap.

**The absent fairness check.** Grey says "I'm not saying you should be ruthless," but the piece
offers no test of the *manager's* side. The signals detect **stalled improvement, not whether
the standard was fair or ever legibly communicated** — a manager who was vague for six months
and then supportive-but-unclear for three will trip all five signals honestly. Signal three
("real support was offered") is the only one pointing back at the manager, and it is
self-assessed. Worth pairing with a second reader before acting.

  tension. That note argues for absorbing friction from an unconventional high performer; this
  one for time-bounding tolerance. They resolve on **trajectory rather than friction**: a
  maverick is difficult and moving, the case here is difficult and static. The failure is
  applying either lens by temperament rather than by evidence.

  about being able to *report* failure without blame; it is not a claim that outcomes never
  carry consequences. Grey's five signals concern a pattern that survived support, which is a
  different object from a single intelligent failure.
- [The Engineering Manager Role](engineering-manager-role.md) — the People Leadership pillar

- [Raising Problems Without Complaining](raising-problems-without-complaining.md) — the same

  signalling discomfort and hoping it registers.

  decision while the cost compounds quietly. The quiet death of a project and indefinite
  coaching mode share a structure: no decision is ever taken, so nothing is ever learned from
  it.
